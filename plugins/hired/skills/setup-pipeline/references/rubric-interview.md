# Rubric interview

The scoring rubric is the whole product. Everything else is plumbing. A rubric that just
says "score fit from 1 to 5" produces scores nobody trusts and the pipeline gets ignored
within a week.

Your job in this interview is to extract the judgment the user already applies when they
look at a posting and decide in four seconds whether to keep reading. They have that
judgment. They have almost certainly never written it down.

## How to run it

Ask one group at a time. Reflect answers back in your own words and let them correct you.
When an answer is vague, ask for the specific example behind it. "I want a good culture"
becomes usable only once you learn it means "I have been burned by companies with no
design function."

Write nothing into the rubric that the user did not say. An invented dimension will feel
right during setup and quietly distort every score afterward.

## 0. Collect real postings first

Before any questions:

> "Paste me 3 to 5 job postings that are the kind of thing you are going after. Links or
> the text, either is fine. Then one or two you looked at and decided against."

Read all of them properly. The wants and the passes are both load bearing, and the passes
are often more informative, because what someone rules out is more specific than what they
say they want.

What to pull out:

- **Common threads they did not name.** Stage, company size, product surface, remote
  policy, whether the role owns revenue. If four of five postings share something, ask
  whether it matters or is an accident of where they were looking.
- **Stated preferences with no supporting evidence.** If they say seniority is critical
  but the examples span three levels, ask about it. Usually the stated version is
  aspirational and the examples are true.
- **The line between the wants and the passes.** Ask directly: "what made you skip this
  one?" The answer is frequently a hard filter they had not articulated, and it is the
  single most useful sentence in the whole interview.
- **Vocabulary.** Use the words their target postings use. A rubric written in the
  industry's language scores more accurately than one written in yours.

Ask about anything the postings contradict. Do not quietly resolve it yourself: the
contradiction is information, and the user is the one who knows which side is real.

Keep two of these postings aside. They become the calibration anchors in section 6.

If they have no examples to hand, skip this and run the interview on questions alone. Say
that the rubric will drift more in the first couple of weeks and will need correcting.

## 1. Dimensions

> "When you look at a posting and decide it is worth reading, what are you actually
> checking?"

Let them list freely, then push for 4 to 7 dimensions. Typical ones: domain or industry
fit, seniority and scope, location, technical depth expected, company stage or size,
product surface (user-facing vs internal vs infrastructure), mission.

For each dimension, capture:

- What a **strong** signal looks like, in concrete terms.
- What a **weak** signal looks like.
- Whether it is a filter (fails means capped) or a spectrum (fails means points off).

Then ask which two matter most. You do not need numeric weights. You need to know what
breaks a tie.

## 2. Hard filters

> "What makes a role dead on arrival, no matter how perfect the rest of it is?"

Location, comp floor, level, industries they will not touch, on-site requirements, travel,
companies they will not work for. Get the exact boundary, not the shape: not "too far
away" but "outside a 45 minute commute from home unless the role is fully remote."

Write each one as a rule the scorer can apply mechanically, and record what the cap is
(usually Very Low, sometimes Low).

## 3. Standout logic

This is the highest leverage question in the interview and the one most people have never
considered.

> "Forget whether you are qualified. What kinds of roles would you be an unusually
> interesting candidate for, compared to everyone else who applies?"

Probe for:

- Interests outside their job that are real and hard to fake. A degree in something
  unrelated, a hobby pursued seriously, a community they are part of, a place they know
  deeply.
- Unusual combinations in their background. Two industries most people never pair.
- Things they build or follow on their own time that are ahead of their resume.

Then:

> "Is there a type of role that sits at the intersection of two or more of those? What
> would it look like?"

If they can name two or three independent standout dimensions, write a rule for how the
score responds to how many a role hits. Something like: hits all of them means a Very
High floor, hits two means a High floor, hits one means score normally, hits none means
capped at Medium unless the company itself is exceptional.

Be careful with that cap. It is powerful and it is how a pipeline stops recommending
generic roles, but it will also suppress perfectly good ordinary opportunities. Say that
out loud and let the user decide. Some people want the cap. Some want a nudge instead.

If they have no standout angle, do not manufacture one. Skip this section and lean harder
on the dimensions.

## 4. Company signals

> "What do you want to know about a company before you would take the interview?"

Stage, size, funding, profitability, remote policy, who the founders are, whether the
product is one they would use, layoff history, reputation. Ask whether any of these are
filters or just context for the summary.

Ask if they want to keep a tier list of specific companies. If yes, seed it now with the
ones they can name off the top of their head, and note that it lives in the config page
and can grow. A tier list is the fastest way to make scores feel personal.

## 5. Tier definitions and calibration

> "Out of 20 postings that come through, how many should be Very High?"

The answer is usually one, sometimes zero. Then work down. The point of this question is
that they define scarcity before seeing any scores, which is the only way the tiers stay
meaningful.

Then write each tier in their words. A useful set answers: what does this tier mean I
should do? A tier that does not imply an action is decoration. Suggest tying each tier to
one of: tailor a full application, send a generic application, watch, ignore.

## 6. Calibration test

Before saving, run this. It catches more problems than everything above.

1. Take the two postings you set aside in section 0: one they would apply to, one they
   passed on. If they had no examples, ask for them now.
2. Score both against the draft rubric and show your reasoning dimension by dimension.
   Score them cold, without referring back to what they told you about these specific
   postings, or you will just be reciting their opinion back to them.
3. Ask whether the scores and the reasoning match their gut.

When they disagree, the rubric is wrong, not their gut. Find the missing dimension or the
mis-set filter and fix it. One round of this is usually enough. Two is common.

Save the two calibration examples into the rubric with their scores. They act as anchors
for future runs and make it obvious later if scoring has drifted.
